The program

Three things we build, one meeting at a time

Speaking skills only stick when students feel safe enough to try. So we build confidence first, then technique, then the thinking that wins a debate.

Confidence

Low-pressure games make it normal to stand up, make mistakes and keep going.

  • Speak in front of a group without freezing
  • Shake off a mistake and keep talking
  • Give and take kind, useful feedback

Public speaking

The craft of a good talk: a clear structure, a strong voice and body language that backs you up.

  • Open with a hook and close with a point
  • Use pace, pauses and volume on purpose
  • Cut the “um”s and keep eye contact

Speech & debate

How to build an argument, answer the other side and think on your feet.

  • Make a claim, back it up, say why it matters
  • Listen closely and respond, not just react
  • Speak impromptu with one minute to prepare

The skill path

Five levels from first words to full debate

Students move up when they're ready, not by age. Each level ends with a challenge they complete in front of their chapter and a badge to show for it. Finish all five to earn a Future Voices certificate.

  1. Level 1

    Spark

    Get comfortable being seen and heard. Name games, voice warm-ups and 30-second talks about things you love.

    Challenge: a 30-second “all about me” talk
  2. Level 2

    Story

    Give a talk a shape: a beginning that hooks, a middle that makes sense and an ending people remember.

    Challenge: a 2-minute personal story
  3. Level 3

    Stage

    Use your voice and body on purpose: pace, pauses, gestures and eye contact that holds a room.

    Challenge: a 3-minute persuasive speech
  4. Level 4

    Spar

    Learn to argue well: claim, warrant and impact, plus rebuttals and respectful cross-examination.

    Challenge: a full practice debate round
  5. Level 5

    Showcase

    Bring it all together at a Future Voices showcase for families, schools and other chapters.

    Challenge: speak or debate at the showcase

    Chapters

    Bring Future Voices to your school or community

    A chapter is a local Future Voices club. It can meet at a school, library, community center or place of worship. Students run it, an adult supports it, and we give you everything you need to start.

    Students

    Chapter lead

    A student who wants to start the club. You recruit members, run meetings with the playbook and help each other level up.

    Adults

    Chapter advisor

    A teacher, coach, librarian or parent who supervises meetings and helps book a room. No speech or debate experience needed.

    Feed the Monster

    Program team

    We train new leads, send the activity playbook, check in each month and connect chapters for showcases and friendly debates.

    Start a chapter in four steps

    1. Apply

      Fill in the form. It takes about five minutes. We reply within a week.

    2. Find an advisor and a room

      Ask a teacher or trusted adult to sponsor the chapter, and pick a regular time and place to meet.

    3. Get trained

      Join a short online kickoff call with the Future Voices team and get the chapter playbook.

    4. Hold your first meeting

      Invite at least five members and run the ready-made first meeting. Your chapter then appears in the directory.

      Questions

      Good questions, short answers

      Who is Future Voices for?

      Young people in elementary, middle and high school. Chapters can be one age group or mixed. The activities work for shy first-timers and for students who already love to talk.

      I get really nervous speaking. Is this for me?

      Yes. Most students start out nervous. Level 1 is built for that: short turns, small groups and games where nobody is judged. Confidence comes from lots of small, safe reps.

      Is this the same as a debate team?

      Not quite. Future Voices teaches debate, but it starts with confidence and everyday speaking. Students who want to compete will be ready for speech and debate tournaments after the Spar level.

      Does the adult advisor need debate experience?

      No. The playbook explains every activity step by step. The advisor's main job is to supervise, keep meetings safe and cheer students on.

      How many students do we need to start?

      Five is enough to run every activity. Many chapters start small and grow once friends see how fun the meetings are.

      What is Feed the Monster?

      Feed the Monster is the organization behind Future Voices. We run Future Voices, train chapter leads and connect chapters with each other.
